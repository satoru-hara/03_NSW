
# Human Natural Structure Framework Foundational Specification HNS‑36  
## Cross‑Domain Structure

---

## 0. Cross‑Domain Structure Overview

### 1) Definition of Cross‑Domain Structure
The Cross‑Domain Structure defines how HNS‑36 unifies multiple human‑related domains—cognition, behavior, interaction, environment, systems, and civilization—into a single structural coordinate system.  
It integrates:  
- **Human Natural Layers** (causal structure; vertical axis)  
- **Abstract Cognitive Categories** (explanatory structure; horizontal axis)

By intersecting these two axes, HNS‑36 produces a **36‑cell structural matrix** that allows any human or societal phenomenon to be positioned consistently within the same framework.

### 2) Purpose and Significance of Cross‑Domain Structure
The purpose of the Cross‑Domain Structure is to:  
- Provide a unified coordinate system for modeling human and social phenomena  
- Separate causal structure (how humans operate) from explanatory structure (how we interpret them)  
- Enable cross‑domain consistency, allowing individual, organizational, societal, and civilizational processes to be analyzed using the same structural reference

---

## 1. Human Natural Layers

### 1) Overview
Human Natural Layers represent the **six causal layers of the HumanOS**, describing how human beings function as a structured system.  
These layers form the **vertical axis** of the HNS‑36 matrix.  
Each layer defines a distinct causal domain of human operation, from physical foundations to long‑term behavioral patterns.

### 2) Structure

#### (1) PhysicalOS
The physical and biological foundation of human existence.  
Includes sensory systems, motor functions, physiological constraints, and bodily load.

#### (2) CognitiveOS
Internal cognitive processes such as memory, reasoning, interpretation, and internal modeling.  
Defines how humans understand, evaluate, and decide.

#### (3) InteractionOS
Human‑to‑human and human‑to‑environment interaction processes.  
Covers communication, coordination, cooperation, conflict, and social signaling.

#### (4) EnvironmentOS
The external environment in which humans operate.  
Includes physical surroundings, social structures, cultural norms, institutions, and technological contexts.

#### (5) LoadOS
Cognitive load, task load, fatigue, resource allocation, and performance limits.  
Determines how much processing a human can sustain and where breakdowns occur.

#### (6) PatternOS
Long‑term behavioral patterns, habits, routines, and cultural or organizational tendencies.  
Represents stable, repeated structures in human behavior.

---

## 2. Abstract Cognitive Categories

### 1) Overview
Abstract Cognitive Categories define the **six explanatory axes** used to interpret human phenomena at different levels of abstraction.  
These categories form the **horizontal axis** of the HNS‑36 matrix.  
They determine how a phenomenon is explained—at the level of civilization, core principles, modules, applications, systems, or external interfaces.

### 2) Structure

#### (1) Civilization Categories
The highest level of abstraction.  
Covers civilization, culture, historical context, collective values, and macro‑level meaning structures.

#### (2) Core Categories
Fundamental principles, essential structures, and foundational concepts.  
Defines the core logic or essence of a phenomenon.

#### (3) Module Categories
Functional units, components, and modular elements.  
Used to decompose complex structures into identifiable parts.

#### (4) Application Categories
Practical use cases, applied contexts, and real‑world implementations.  
Explains how a structure is used or manifested in practice.

#### (5) System Categories
Integrated systems composed of multiple interacting components.  
Includes organizations, institutions, workflows, and architectures.

#### (6) External Categories
Interfaces, boundary conditions, and external connections.  
Describes how a structure interacts with other systems or environments.
